Your Eyes Have Become Dirty

This essay first appeared in miniskirt magazine in December 2025.

I want to talk about Yohji Yamamoto’s storied statements on the way in which young people think and dress: “Let me talk like an old man. Young people, be careful. Beautiful things are disappearing every day. Be careful.…You don’t need to be [shopping at fast-fashion stores], especially young people. They are beautiful naturally, because they are young. So they should even wear simple jeans and a T-shirt. It’s enough. Don’t be too much fashionable.…The brand advertising is making you crazy. You don’t need to be too sexy. You are sexy enough.” I take this as a warning - as I imagine it was intended - particularly the last line: “You are sexy enough.” He said it in 2012. Were we already too sexy then? From his generational standpoint, I think we were, and I find that frightening to think about.
I recently started wearing jeans and a t-shirt in reaction to his statements. I had read them several times over the past five to seven years on the artificial lake of the internet, but, at twenty-six, they are landing differently. I’ve felt that it’s time to do something - to practice seeing myself as beautiful rather than stalling, too good to fish for compliments. It occurred to me for the first time some months ago that this entire endeavour could be less of a thought experiment than a regimen - a set of actions (for instance, wearing jeans and a t-shirt). I recognized that tasks were at the centre of Yamamoto’s pep talk, and the results have been astonishing - but that is a personal matter.
What does this have to do with the claim that “Beautiful things are disappearing every day?” Elizaveta Federmesser has an intriguing analysis of the positionality of The Row as a fashion company selling luxury basics. Positionality, she suggests, is actually the main thing about The Row, as a brand that relentlessly shares its own perspectives on what it’s doing as part of its marketing strategy. As the label has grown, she writes, “the items stayed in the basics range, [but] the pictures on Instagram consistently referred to something the brand did not provide. By adding chic accessories, Moroccan rugs, designer furniture, collector’s art pieces, and other souvenirs of intellectualized lifestyle, they were elevating away from impending basicness by association.”
The implication is that The Row naturalized a vision of what it offered that was actually quite contrary to truth. Is this synonymous with marketing? I don’t think so. There is another word for it: peddling. This is evidently not what Yamamoto is talking about when he talks about wearing a t-shirt, but very interestingly, The Row ‘started with a quest for a perfect white t-shirt,’ according to Federmesser and others.
The Row may not have been the first company to bank on an affectation, but the positionality that it chose to adorn in relation to that basic item of clothing naturalizes an idea of the white t-shirt as a nuanced subject. The white t-shirt as a nuanced subject rather overshadows, in an intellectualizing move, what might be considered another nuanced subject, i.e. the wearer. I don’t really like this, but maybe it’s good. I have a much greater appetite for an idea of fashion that gives way to the body. There is no perfect white t-shirt. Every white t-shirt that a (young) person wants to wear is beautiful.
I don’t read this as an idealization of youth because I come from a cultural context where the old are expected to dress more extravagantly and more wonderfully than the young. One of the things that age can represent in the version of my home region that I know is an invitation to ornament yourself more lavishly. The existence of this threshold is anti-ageist. It places a soft barrier between age groups - a gradation - such that ideology finds it difficult to hoard the totality of life’s variety and fruits into the span of early youth. It doesn’t matter what you look like - not when it comes to wearing what you want. But if you’re a person of a certain age, then you should explore the prospect of wearing what you felt was too much when you were younger.
The idea here is of course, in contradistinction to The Row, that you can have everything - just not all at once.
